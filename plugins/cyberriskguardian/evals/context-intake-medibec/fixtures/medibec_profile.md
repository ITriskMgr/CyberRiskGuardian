# MediBec profile (fictional teaching case)

Excerpt of sections 3 to 5 of *MediBec Integrated Cybersecurity Teaching Case v2.0b*, M.-A. Léger. Fictional. CC BY-NC 4.0. Used as an evaluation fixture.

3. MediBec Background and Healthcare Profile
MediBec is a fictional private medical centre headquartered in Montreal. It operates 10 clinics across the regions of Montreal, Quebec City, Gatineau, and Saguenay. Its mission is to deliver high-quality healthcare services supported by modern technology, efficient clinical workflows, digital communication, and secure access to patient information.
MediBec holds and processes sensitive personal and medical information, including electronic health records, billing details, administrative records, and data generated by medical equipment and specialized departmental systems. Its technology environment supports clinical care, laboratory management, radiology, pharmacy, billing, online access, and collaboration among medical professionals.
MediBec’s challenge is not simply to prevent cyberattacks. It must protect patient health information, maintain clinical continuity, preserve public trust, comply with privacy and health-sector expectations, and ensure that technology supports care delivery rather than creating unsafe dependencies.

Clinical and operational dependency
The clinic network depends heavily on central technology services. Medical staff rely on access to EHR data, diagnostic information, communications tools, billing systems, medical imagery, and local devices. Even if an attack does not directly harm patients, an outage or data integrity issue can affect appointment scheduling, clinical decision-making, continuity of care, referrals, billing, and confidence in the organization.
For the incident response exercise, students should consider that technical containment decisions may have clinical consequences. For the audit exercise, students should consider whether evidence exists at both the head office and clinic levels. For the risk management exercise, students should consider whether risk tolerance differs between administrative systems and systems directly affecting patient care.
4. Governance, Technology, and Cybersecurity Operating Model
Governance model
Before the revised planning horizon, MediBec treated cybersecurity primarily as an IT operational responsibility. The case now frames cybersecurity as an enterprise risk issue involving patient safety, privacy, clinical continuity, executive oversight, vendor management, and regulatory exposure. The CIO remains accountable for IT strategy and operations, but cybersecurity risk must also be visible to executive management and the Board or ownership group.
For teaching purposes, assume that MediBec has appointed a Cybersecurity and Privacy Steering Committee chaired by an executive sponsor. The CIO continues to lead technology strategy, while the cybersecurity team, privacy/compliance function, clinical operations leaders, and clinic managers share responsibility for risk identification, incident escalation, and remediation.

Technology environment

Case facts and implications
Network infrastructure
Centralized network with primary hub in Montreal, WAN connectivity to clinics, high-speed fibre links, firewalls, IDS/IPS, and antivirus/antimalware controls. Students should assess segmentation, logging, wireless security, and monitoring coverage.
Data centres and storage
Main data centre at the Montreal head office, local clinic storage units, on-site and off-site backups, and encrypted storage for patient, billing, and administrative data. Students should assess backup isolation, recovery testing, data locality, and exposure of local storage.
Cloud integration
Remote access and cloud-based data access support staff and clinicians. Students should consider cloud configuration, identity, logging, shared responsibility, and data residency assumptions.
End-user and clinical devices
Workstations, tablets, handheld devices, and interconnected medical equipment support EHR access and patient data updates. Students should assess endpoint security, device inventory, patching, and device identity.
Software systems
Integrated EHR, radiology, laboratory management, pharmacy, billing, communications, and administrative systems. Students should assess access control, secure configuration, data integrity, and application monitoring.
Communication systems
Voice, video, and messaging services connect clinics and clinicians. Students should consider confidentiality, availability, incident communications, and secure collaboration during outages.
Cybersecurity operating model
The original case describes a lean 15-person IT department, including a two-person cybersecurity team. This staffing level creates a realistic constraint for a private healthcare organization with sensitive data and geographically distributed clinics. Students should not assume that MediBec can implement every control immediately. They must prioritize according to patient safety, privacy impact, operational continuity, and risk reduction per dollar.

Budget context
The original case states that MediBec’s total IT budget, including salaries, is CAD 100 million. For the revised teaching case, assume that the organization is preparing a cybersecurity improvement roadmap for 2024-2030. Students may recommend phased investments, but they should respect non-financial constraints such as staffing capacity, clinical change management, vendor availability, medical-device certification constraints, and the need to maintain care delivery during implementation.

5. Cyber Threat Context: Known Facts and Initial Uncertainties
MediBec has observed multiple unsuccessful cyber-attack attempts since 2020, with an increase in attack attempts in recent months. No successful breach has been confirmed in the baseline case. This is useful pedagogically because students are not only reacting to an incident that already occurred; they are also asked to assess whether MediBec is sufficiently prepared before an incident becomes successful.
For the incident response module, instructors may convert the threat context into a simulated live incident. The simulation should begin with uncertainty: a suspicious authentication event, an abnormal EHR access pattern, unusual traffic from a clinic, a report of failed backups, or a patient complaint about an online service. Students should have to decide what evidence is needed, when to escalate, and how to balance containment with care delivery.
Known fact
Uncertainty students must address
Multiple unsuccessful attack attempts have occurred since 2020.
Were the attempts detected by controls, reported by users, or discovered after the fact? Are logs sufficient to prove they were unsuccessful?
Attack attempts have increased in recent months.
Is the increase due to real threat escalation, improved detection, a targeted campaign, exposed services, or normal background scanning?
MediBec stores sensitive patient and medical data.
Which systems contain the most sensitive data? Where are copies, exports, backups, logs, and local clinic records stored?
MediBec uses cloud access and remote data retrieval.
Are cloud configurations, access controls, logging, and shared-responsibility assumptions understood and tested?
Medical equipment is interconnected with the EHR environment.
Are device inventories complete, patch levels known, and network segments properly separated?
The cybersecurity team is small.
Which controls can realistically be operated internally, which require managed services, and which risks must be accepted temporarily?
Regulatory, privacy, and clinical teaching assumptions
For teaching purposes, assume that MediBec must consider Quebec and Canadian privacy expectations, health information confidentiality, professional obligations, contractual obligations with technology and cloud providers, and evidence preservation if a major incident occurs. If the instructor wishes to use a different jurisdictional variant, this section can be adapted.
The purpose of this case is not to train students to provide legal advice. Students should identify privacy, clinical, contractual, and regulatory issues, explain why they matter, and recommend that MediBec coordinate with legal counsel, privacy officers, clinical leadership, relevant authorities, insurers, and external forensic experts as appropriate.
